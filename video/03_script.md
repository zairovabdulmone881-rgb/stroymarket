## A Company In Crisis

To understand the Boston Tea Party, we have to start in London, with a company.

The East India Company was founded by royal charter in the year sixteen hundred. By the 1770s, it had its own army and ruled large parts of India.

After the Battle of Plassey in 1757, it gained power in Bengal. In 1765, it received the right to collect the region's tax revenue.

Then, in 1769 and 1770, a catastrophic famine struck Bengal and killed millions of people.

By 1772, the Company was heading toward bankruptcy, even while it kept paying dividends to its shareholders.


## A Company In Crisis

In 1773, Parliament lent the Company one point four million pounds to keep it afloat.

And its warehouses in London were packed with roughly seventeen million pounds of tea that nobody was buying.

Britain was also deep in debt after the Seven Years' War. Its national debt had almost doubled. The government wanted the Company to survive, and it wanted revenue from America.


## Taxation Without Consent

Parliament's answer was the Tea Act, passed in May 1773.

It allowed the Company to sell tea directly to the American colonies, without going through London wholesalers and without paying the usual British export duty.

The tea would be cheaper than the smuggled Dutch tea many colonists drank, even after the tax.


## Taxation Without Consent

But that tax was the problem. In 1767, Parliament had placed duties on glass, lead, paint, paper and tea imported into the colonies.

After colonial boycotts, most of those duties were repealed in 1770. The tax on tea stayed.

For many colonists, paying it meant accepting that Parliament could tax them without their consent. And the Company's special advantage threatened to push colonial merchants out of the tea trade.

So a plan meant to rescue a company in London became a political crisis in America.


## A Town On Edge

Boston was already on edge. In 1765, colonists organized boycotts and street protests against the Stamp Act, and Parliament backed down the following year.

In March 1770, British soldiers fired into a crowd in Boston and killed five colonists, an event known as the Boston Massacre.

And in 1772, Boston created a Committee of Correspondence, a network meant to share news and coordinate resistance with other towns.


## The Crossing

In the autumn of 1773, the Company sent tea ships to four colonial ports: Boston, New York, Philadelphia and Charleston.

Local committees pressured the agents who were supposed to receive the tea, and in several ports those agents resigned.

In Philadelphia, the ship was sent back to Britain. In Charleston, the tea was unloaded and locked in a warehouse. It was later sold by revolutionary authorities to help fund the war.

But Boston was different.


## Boston Refuses

In Boston, the agents receiving the tea included two sons of the royal governor, Thomas Hutchinson.

The first ship, the Dartmouth, arrived on November 28th. Volunteers stood guard on the wharf day and night to make sure no tea was unloaded.

British law gave the owner twenty days to unload and pay the duty. After that, customs officers could seize the cargo.

The colonists wanted the tea sent back to England. But the governor would not allow the ships to leave without the duty being paid.

On December 16th, with the deadline closing in, thousands of people packed into the Old South Meeting House. The ship's owner, Francis Rotch, was sent to the governor's house in Milton to ask for permission to leave. Hutchinson refused.


## The Harbor

That evening, groups of men walked down to Griffin's Wharf. Some were disguised as Mohawk Indians, with darkened faces and blankets over their shoulders.

A crowd gathered on the wharf to watch. The men were never named at the time, and many stayed silent about it for decades.

They boarded three ships: the Dartmouth, the Eleanor and the Beaver.

Using ropes and hatchets, they hoisted the heavy chests out of the holds, smashed them open, and dumped the tea into the water.

It took about three hours. By the end, three hundred and forty-two chests had been emptied into Boston Harbor.

That was roughly ninety-two thousand pounds of tea, more than forty tons.

Almost nothing else was damaged. A padlock was broken, and the next day it was replaced.


## The Cost

The East India Company put its loss at nine thousand, six hundred and fifty-nine pounds.

The next day, John Adams wrote that the destruction of the tea was so bold, so daring and so firm, that he could not but consider it an “Epocha in History.”

Not everyone agreed. Benjamin Franklin, in London, said the destroyed tea should be paid for.

And the name “Boston Tea Party” came much later. It only became common in the 1830s.


## The Response

Britain's answer came in 1774. Parliament passed a series of laws that the colonists called the Intolerable Acts.

The Boston Port Act closed Boston Harbor to trade until the town paid for the tea.

Another law cut back self-government in Massachusetts. General Thomas Gage, the commander of British troops in America, became governor.

Instead of isolating Boston, the laws pushed the colonies together. Other colonies sent food and money to help the town.


## From Harbor To Revolution

In September 1774, delegates from twelve colonies met in Philadelphia for the First Continental Congress.

On April 19th, 1775, about sixteen months after the tea went into the harbor, British troops and colonial militia exchanged fire at Lexington and Concord.

And on July 4th, 1776, the colonies declared independence.


## Finale

The tea was not the only cause of the revolution. Historians point to a longer chain of disputes over taxation, representation and authority.

But the Tea Act shows how a rescue plan for a failing corporation could help set an empire on a collision course.

The Company itself survived. It kept expanding in India, and it ruled there until 1858, when the British Crown took over.
